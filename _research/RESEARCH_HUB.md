# ⚖️ Legal Research Hub — The Bible

> **The skill you are building is not "finding documents." It is the discipline of *never relying on a proposition you have not verified to its source, and never citing an authority you have not noted up.***
>
> This page is the spine. Everything else in the vault hangs off it.

---

## Part 0 — The Research Mindset (read this first, every time)

Before you touch a database, internalise five rules. They are the difference between a student who "researches" and a lawyer who *knows*.

1. **Primary before secondary — but start with a map.** You cannot find the right primary sources if you do not know the shape of the area. Begin with a secondary source (Halsbury's, a textbook, a good journal article) to understand the doctrine and *which* statute/case governs. Then go to primary.
2. **Note up every case before you rely on it.** A case in your notes is an *opinion*, not necessarily *law*. Check whether it has been reversed, overruled, distinguished, or followed. See [[#Part 4 — Note-Up (the soul skill)]].
3. **Record source + pinpoint for every proposition.** "The rule in *Carlill* is X" is useless. "The rule in *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, 261–262 is X" is research. If you cannot pinpoint it, you have not verified it.
4. **Search both directions.** Once you find authority supporting a position, actively hunt for authority *undermining* it. A one-sided answer is a wrong answer.
5. **Know what you are looking at: primary vs secondary.** A journal article *commenting* on a case is not the case. A statute as passed is not the same as the statute in force (check amendments). Know the difference.

## Part 1 — The Universal Workflow (decision tree)

> Given a legal question, walk this path. Do not skip steps.

```
QUESTION
   │
   ▼
1. DECOMPOSE — What area of law? What jurisdiction? What is the precise
   legal issue? (If you cannot state the issue in one sentence, keep digging.)
   │
   ▼
2. MAP THE AREA (secondary)
   → Halsbury's Laws of Singapore / textbook / journal article
   → Identify the governing STATUTE and the LEADING CASES
   │
   ▼
3. READ THE LEGISLATION (primary)
   → Singapore Statutes Online (in-force version, check amendments)
   → Note the exact section(s)
   │
   ▼
4. READ THE LEADING CASES (primary)
   → Locate full judgment; read ratio, not just headnote
   │
   ▼
5. NOTE UP (is it still good law?)
   → LawNet Citator / KeyCite / Shepard's / LawCite
   → Check: reversed? overruled? distinguished? applied?
   │
   ▼
6. CHECK CURRENT DEVELOPMENTS (current awareness)
   → Recent judgments, law reform, pending appeals
   │
   ▼
7. RECORD WITH PINPOINTS (into your notes/blocks)
   → Every proposition gets: authority + pinpoint + status
```

## Part 3 — Primary vs Secondary (know the difference)

| Type | What it is | Examples |
|------|-----------|----------|
| **Primary — legislation** | The law itself (statutes, subsidiary legislation) | Singapore Statutes Online, eGazette |
| **Primary — case law** | Judicial decisions | LawNet, BAILII, AustLII |
| **Secondary — encyclopaedic** | Authoritative commentary that maps the law | Halsbury's Laws of Singapore |
| **Secondary — textbooks** | Academic treatise | Phang, *The Law of Contract in Singapore* |
| **Secondary — journals** | Scholarly articles | SAL journals, law reviews |
| **Secondary — current awareness** | Recent developments, news | Law Watch, blogs |

**The trap**: citing a secondary source as if it were primary. "Halsbury's says X" is a *finding aid*, not authority. Trace it back to the case or statute Halsbury's cites — and note *that* up.

## Part 4 — Note-Up (the soul skill)

### What it is

**Noting up** = examining how a case has been treated in subsequent cases to determine whether it is still authoritative ("good law"). You check whether it has been:
- **Reversed** on appeal
- **Overruled** by a superior court in a later case
- **Distinguished** (limited to its facts)
- **Followed / applied / considered** (affirmed as good law)

> In the US this is called **Shepardizing** (LexisNexis) or **KeyCite** (Westlaw). In Singapore/UK you use **LawNet Citator**, **KeyCite**, **Shepard's**, **JustisOne**, or the free **LawCite**.

### The Singapore annotation statuses

Singapore judgments annotate how a case was treated. The standard statuses you will see (e.g. in LawNet) include: **followed, applied, considered, distinguished, not followed, overruled, reversed**. Know what each means — a case that is merely "considered" carries far less weight than one "followed" by the Court of Appeal.

### The workflow

```
1. Take the case citation.
2. Run it through LawNet Citator (Singapore) or LawCite (free, common law).
3. Read the "Cases referring to this case" list.
4. Filter: was it OVERRULED or REVERSED? (→ it is NOT good law, or good law
   only on a narrower point)
5. Check the LATEST treatment — a case followed 10 times can still be
   overruled once in 2025. Always read the most recent referring judgment.
6. Record the status in your case brief: e.g. "followed in [X], distinguished
   in [Y], still good law as at [date]".
```

## Part 7 — Legal Abbreviations & Decoding Citations

- **[Cardiff Index to Legal Abbreviations](http://www.legalabbrevs.cardiff.ac.uk/)** — the definitive decoder for law report abbreviations (e.g. "SLR" = Singapore Law Reports, "QB" = Queen's Bench).
- When you see `[2019] 1 SLR 349`, the parts are: **year**, **volume**, **report series**, **page**. Learn to parse this instantly — it is how you find any case in any database.
