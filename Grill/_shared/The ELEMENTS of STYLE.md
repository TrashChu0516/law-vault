## Foreword & Introduction
- What should be easy and flowing looks tangled or feeble or overblown—not what was meant at all. What's wrong with me, each one thinks. Why can't I get this right?
- English prose composition is not only a necessary skill but a sensible pursuit as well—a way to spend one's days
- Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no necessary words, a paragraph no necessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts. This requires not that the writer make all sentences short or avoid all detail and treat subjects only in outline, but that every word tell. 
- Perhaps the book has become something of a curiosity. To me, it still seems to maintain its original poise, standing, in a drafty time, erect, resolute, and assured. I still find the Strunkian wisdom a comfort, the Strunkian humour a delight, and the Stunkian attitude toward right-and-wrong a blessing undisguised. 
- Style rules of this sort are somewhat a matter of individual preference. We should acknowledge the fallacy of inflexibility and the danger of doctrine. The best writers sometimes disregard the rules of rhetoric. When they do so, however, the reader will usually find in the sentence some compensating merit, attained at the cost of violation. Unless he is certain of doing as well, he will probably do best to follow the rules. 

## Elementary Rules of Usage
1. Form the possessive singular of nouns by adding 's.
	1. The prenominal possessives *hers, its, theirs, yours* have no apostrophe. 
	2. Indefinite pronouns use the apostrophe to show possession. 
		1. somebody else's umbrella.
2. In a series of three or more terms with a single conjunction, use a comma after each term except the last.
3. Enclose parenthetic expressions between commas. 
	1. The abbreviations *etc.*, *i.e.*, and *e.g.*, the abbreviations for academic degrees, and titles that follow a name are parenthetic and should be punctuated accordingly. 
		1. Letters, packages, etc., should go there.
	2. Nonrestrictive clauses are parenthetic, as are similar clauses introduced by conjunctions indicating time or place. A nonrestrictive clause is one that does not serve to identify or define the antecedent noun. Restrictive clauses, by contrast, are not parenthetic and are not set off by commas. 
		1. The audience, which had at first been indifferent, became more and more interested.  == The audience was at first indifferent. Later it became more and more interested. 
		2. People who live in glass houses shouldn't throw stones. 
4. Place a comma before a conjunction introducing an independent clause.
	1. The situation is perilous, but if we are prepared to act promptly, there is still one chance of escape. 
	2. I have heard the arguments, but am still unconvinced. By contrast, he has had several years' experience and is thoroughly competent.
5. If two or more clauses grammatically complete and not joined by a conjunction are to form a single sentence, the proper mark of punctuation is a semicolon. 
	1. Note that if the second clause is preceded by an adverb, such as *accordingly, besides, then, therefore*, or *thus*, and not by a conjunction, the semicolon is still required. 
		1. I had never been in the place before; besides, it was dark as a tomb.
6. Do not break sentences in two.
7. Use a colon after an independent clause to introduce a list of particulars, an appositive, an amplification, or an illustrative quotation. 
	1. It usually follows an independent clause and should not separate a verb from its complement or a proposition from its object.
		1. Understanding is that penetrating quality of knowledge that grows from (no colon here!) theory, practice, conviction, assertion, error and humiliation
	2. Join two independent clauses with a colon if the second interprets or amplifies the first.
	3. A colon may introduce a quotation that supports or contributes to the preceding clause.
		1. The squalor of the streets reminder her of a line from Oscar Wilde: "We are all in the gutter, but some of us are looking at the stars."
8. Use a dash to set off an abrupt break or interruption and to announce a long appositive or summary. A dash is a mark of separation stronger than a comma, less formal than a colon, and more relaxed than parentheses. 
	1. The increasing reluctance of the sun to rise, the extra nip in the freeze, the patter of shed leaves dropping—all the evidences of fall drifting into winter were clearer each day. 
9. The number of the subject determines the number of the verb.
	1. Words that intervene between subject and verb do not affect the number of the verb:
		1. The bittersweet flavour of youth—its trials, its joys, its adventures, its challenges—is not soon forgotten. 
	2. A common blunder is the use of a singular verb form in a relative clause following "one of..." or a similar expression when the relative is the subject.
		1. One of the ablest scientists who have attacked this problem.
	3. Use a singular verb form after *each, either, everyone, everybody, neither nobody, someone*.
		1. Although both clocks strike cheerfully, neither keeps a good time.
	4. A plural verb is commonly used when none suggests more than one thing or person.
		1. None are so fallible as those who are sure they are right.
	5. Compounds subjects qualified by *each* or *every* take a singular verb.
		1. Every window, picture, and mirror was smashed.
	6. A singular object remains singular even if other nouns are connected to it by *with, as well as, in addition to, except, together with, and no less than*.
		1. His speech as well as his manner is objectionable. 
	7. A linking verb agrees with the number of its subject.
		1. What is wanted is a few more pairs of hands.
10. Use the proper case of pronoun.
	1. Use the simple personal pronoun as a subject.
		1. Blake and I stayed home.
11. A participial phrase at the beginning of a sentence must refer to the grammatical subject.
	1. On arriving in Chicago, he (the subject) was met at the station by his friends.
## Elementary Principles of Composition
1. Choose a suitable design and hold on to it.
	1. Writing, to be effective, must follow closely the thoughts of the writer, but not necessarily in the order in which those thoughts occur. This calls for a scheme of procedure. In most cases, planning must be a deliberate prelude to writing.
2. Make the paragraph the unit of composition.
	1. As a rule, begin each paragraph either with a sentence that suggests the topic or with a sentence that helps the transition. 
3. Use the active voice—it is usually more direct and vigorous than the passive.
	1. Many a tame sentence of description or exposition can be made lively and emphatic by substituting a transitive in the active voice for some such perfunctory expression as *there is* or *could be heard*,
		1. The reason he left college was that his health became impaired --> Failing health compelled him to leave college. 
		2. It was not long before she was very sorry that she had said what she had --> She soon repented her words.
	2. When a sentence is made stronger, it usually becomes shorter. Thus, brevity is a by-product of vigor.
4. Put statements in positive form. (STEVEN: Legal nuance overrides the strict application of this rule)
	1. Make definite assertions. Avoid tame, colorless, hesitating, noncommittal language. Use the word *not* as a means of denial or in antithesis, never as a means of evasion
	2. The reader is dissatisfied with being told only what is not; the reader wishes to be told that is. Hence it is better to express even a negative in positive form. Use:
		1. Dishonest instead of not honest;
		2. Trifling instead of not important;
		3. Ignore instead of did not pay attention to;
		4. Forgot instead of did not remember;
		5. Distrusted instead of did not have much confidence in.
	3. Statements qualified with unnecessary auxiliaries or conditionals sound irresolute.
	4. If your every sentence admits a doubt, your writing will lack authority. Save the auxiliaries *would, should, could, may, might,* and *can* for situations involving real uncertainty.
5. Use definite, specific, and concrete language.
	1. Prefer the specific to the general, the definite to the vague, the concrete to the abstract.
		1. *It rained every day for a week* instead of *A period of unfavourable weather set in*.
	2.  In exposition and in argument, the writer must likewise never lose hold of the concrete; and even when dealing with general principles, the writer must furnish particular instances of their application
6. **Omit needless words. (STEVEN: Mentioned by my LRW Prof + the very rule that compelled me to look up this book)**
	1. Vigorous writing is concise. Every word must tell.
		1. *Whether* instead of *the question as to whether*
		2. *no doubt(doubtless)* instead of *there is no doubt but that*
		3. *he* instead of *he is a man who*
		4. *this subject* instead of *this is a subject that*
		5. *because* instead of *the reason why is that*
	2. The fact that is an especially debilitating expression. It should be revised out of every sentence in which it occurs.
		1. *since* instead of *owing to the fact that*
		2. *though* instead of *in spite of the fact that*
		3. *remind you* instead of *call your attention to the fact that*
		4. *I was unaware that* instead of *I was unaware of the fact that*
	3. *Who is, which was*, and the like are often superflous.
		1. His cousin, (*who is*) a member of the same firm,
7. Avoid a succession of loose sentences.
	1. A write who had written a series of loose sentences should recast enough of them to remove the monotony, replacing them with simple sentences, sentences of two clauses joined by a semicolon.
8. Express coordinate ideas in similar form.
	1. By this principle, an article or a preposition applying to all the members of a series must either be used only before the first term or else be repeated before each term.
	2. Some words require a particular preposition in certain idiomatic uses. When such words are joined in a compound construction, all the appropriate prepositions must be included, unless they are the same.
	3. Correlative expressions (*both, and; not, but; not only, but also; either, or; first, second, third;*) should be followed by the same grammatical construction. Many violations of this rule can be corrected by rearranging the sentence.
		1. A time not for words but for action.
		2. You must either grant his request or incur his ill will.
		3. My objections are, first, that the measure is unjust; second, that it is unconstitutional. 
9. Keep related words together.
	1. The position of the words in a sentence is the principal means of showing their relationship. Confusion and ambiguity result when words are badly placed. **The writer must, therefore, bring together the words and the groups of the words that are related and keep apart those that are not related.**
		1. *For just two dollars you can call your mother in London and tell her all about George's taking you out to dinner.* INSTEAD OF *You can call your mother in London and tell her all about George's taking you out to dinner for just two dollars*.
		2. *New York's first commercial human-sperm bank opened Friday when semen samples were taken from eighteen man. The samples were then frozen and stored in a stainless steel tank.* INSTEAD OF *with semen sampls from eighteen man frozen in a stainless steel tank*.
	2. The subject of a sentence and the principal verb should not, as a rule, be separated by a phrase or clause that can be transferred to the beginning. 
	3. **The relative pronoun should come, in most instances, immediately after its antecedent.**
		1. A stir that suggested disapproval swept the audience. INSTEAD OF There was a stir in the audience that suggested disapproval.
	4. **Modifiers should come, if possible, next to the words they modify. If several expressions modify the same word, they should be arranged so that no wrong relation is suggested.** 
		1. At a meeting of the committee yesterday, the director said he hoped all members would give generously to the fund. INSTEAD OF The director said he hoped all members would give generously to the Fund at a meeting of the committee yesterday. 
10. In summaries, keep to one tense. (STEVEN: Specific legal requirements override this rule.)
	1. If the summary is in the present tense, antecedent action should be expressed by the perfect; if in the past, by the past perfect. Shifting from one tense to another gives the appearance of uncertainty and irresolution.
11. Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end. (STEVEN: SUBJECT TO IRAC AND CEPAC)
	1. The proper place in the sentence for the word or group of words that the writer desires to make most prominent is usually the end. 
		1. The word or group of words entitled to this position of prominence is usually the logical predicate—that is, the *new* element in the sentence.
	2. The other prominent position in the sentence is the beginning. Any element in the sentence other than the subject becomes emphatic when placed first.
		1. Deceit or treachery she could never forgive.
	3. The principle that **the proper place for what is to be made most prominent is the end** applies equally to the words of a sentence, to the sentences of a paragraph, and to the paragraphs of a composition

## A Few Matters of Form
1. Colloquialisms. If you use s colloquialism, simply use it; do not draw attention to it by enclosing it in quotation marks.
2. Exclamations. Do not attempt to emphasise simple statements by using a mark of exclamation. 
3. Headings. Omit the period after a title or heading.
4. Hyphen. When two or more words are combined to form a compound adjective, a hyphen is usually required. 
5. Numerals. Do not spell out dates or other serial numbers. Write them in figures or in Roman notation, as appropriate.
	1. August 9, 1988. Part XII. Rule 3. 352d Infantry.
6. Parentheses. A sentence containing an expression in parentheses is punctuated outside the last mark of parenthesis exactly as if the parenthetical expression where absent. The expression within the marks is punctuated as if it stood by itself.
7. Quotations. (American English)
	1. Formal quotations cited as documentary evidence are introduced by a colon and enclosed by quotation marks.
	2. A quotation grammatically in apposition or the direct object of a verb is preceded by a comma and enclosed in quotation marks. 
		1. I am reminded of the advice of my neighbour, "Never worry about your heart till it stops beating."
		2. Mark Twain says, "A classic is something that everybody wants to have read and nobody wants to read."
	3. When a quotation is followed by an attributive phrase, the comma is enclosed within the quotation marks.
		1. "I can't attend," she said.
	4. Quotations introduced by *that* are indirect discourse and not enclosed in quotation marks.
		1. Dickinson states that a coffin is a small domain.
	5. Proverbial expressions and familiar phrases of literary origin require no quotation marks
8. Titles. For the titles of literary works, scholarly usage prefers italics with capitalised initials.

## Words and Expressions Commonly Misused
1. An allusion is an indirect mention,  a reference is a specific one.
2. Alternate means every other one in a series; alternative means one of two possibilities:
	1. As the flooded road left no alternative, they took the alternate route. 
3. Use expect instead of anticipate in the sense of simple expectation. 
4. Particularly awkward is *but* closely following another, thus making a contract to a contrast, or a reservation to reservation. This is easily corrected by rearrangement
5. To *compare to* is to point out or imply resemblances between objects regarded as essentially of a different order; to *compare with* is mainly to point out differences between objects regarded as essentially of the same order.
	1. Life has been compared to a pilgrimage, to a drama, to a battle;
	2. Congress may be compared with the British Parliament.
6. A zoo comprises animals. Animals constitute the zoo.
7. Do not *contact* people; get in touch with them, look them up, phone them, find them, or meet them.
8. *Cope* is an intransitive verb used with with.
9. *Etc.* Literally, "and other things". In formal writing, *etc.* is a misfit. An item enough to call for *etc.* is probably important enough to be named.
10. Avoid starting with *however* when the meaning is "nevertheless". The word usually serves better when not in first position. When *however* comes first, it means "in whatever way" or "to whatever extent".
11. *Imply*. *Infer*. (Steven: Important in law)
	1. Something implied is something suggested or indicated, though not expressed.
	2. Something inferred is something deduced from evidence at hand.
12. *In terms of*. A piece of padding usually best omitted.
	1. The job was unattractive in terms of salary --> The salary made the job unattractive.
13. Nothing becomes *interesting* or *funny* by being labelled so.
14. *Less* refers to quantity, *fewer* to number.
15. *Meaningful*. A bankrupt adjective. Choose another, or rephrase.
	1. We are instituting many meaningful changes in the curriculum. --> We are improving the curriculum in many ways.
16. *Nor*. Often used wrongly for *or* after negative impressions.
	1. He cannot eat or sleep.
	2. He cannot ear nor can he sleep.
17. *Participle for verbal noun*. Any sentence in which the use of possessive is awkward or impossible should be recast.
	1. There was great dissatisfaction with the decision of the arbitrators being favourable to the company. --> There was great dissatisfaction with the arbitrators' decision in favour of the company. 
18.  From *the people* comes political support or organisation; from *the public* comes artistic appreciation or commercial patronage. 
19. Use *first, second, third*; do not prettify numbers with -*ly*.
20. Any sentence with *than* to express comparison should be examined to make sure no essential words are missing.
	1. "I am closer to my mother than to my father" versus "I am closer to my mother than my father is".
21. *That. Which.* *That* is the defining, or restrictive, pronoun, *which* die nondefining, or nonrestrictive.
	1. The lawn mower that is broken is in the garage. (Tells which one).
	2. That lawn mower, which is broken, is in the garage. (Adds a fact about the only mower is question).
22. If you feel you are possessed of the truth, or of the fact, simply state it. Do not give it advance billing such as "*the truth is*" or "*the fact is*".
23. The pronoun *this*, referring to the complete sense of preceding sentence or clause, can't always carry the load and so may produce an imprecise statement. Do specify.
24. *Transpire* means *become known*, and not "happen". 
	1. Eventually, the grim account of his villainy transpired. 
25. *Very.* Use this word sparingly. Where emphasis is necessary, use words strong in themselves.
26. *While* can often be replaced by a semicolon. Use only with strict literalness, in the sense of "during the time that".

## An Approach to Style
1. There is no infallible guide to good writing, no assurance that a person who thinks clearly will be able to write clearly. 
2. Young writers often suppose that style is a garnish to the meat of prose, a sauce by which a dull dish is made possible. Style has no such separate identity; it is nondetachable, unfilterable. The approach to style is by way of plainness, simplicity, orderliness, sincerity. 
3. The mind travels faster than the pen; consequently, writing becomes a question of learning to make occasional wing shots, bringing down the bird of thought as it flashes by. 
4.  Place yourself in the background. Write in a way that draws the reader's attention to the sense and substance of the writing, rather than to the mood and temper of the author. 
5. Never imitate consciously, but do not worry about being an imitator; take pains instead to admire what is good. Then when you write in a way naturally, you will echo the halloos that bear repeating.
6. Before beginning to compose something, gauge the nature and extent of the enterprise and work from a suitable design.
7. Write with nouns and verbs, not with adjectives and adverbs. In general, it is nouns and verbs, not their assistants, that give good writing its toughness and colour.
8. Revise and rewrite. Revising is part of writing. Above all, do not be afraid to experiment with what you have written. Save both the original and revised editions. 
9. Do not overwrite. Rich and ornate prose is hard to digest, generally unwholesome, and sometimes nauseating.  
10. Do not overstate. A single overstatement, wherever or however it occurs, diminishes the whole, and a single carefree superlative has the power to destroy, for readers, the object of your enthusiasm.
11. Avoid the use of qualifiers like *rather, very, little, pretty*—these are the leeches that infest the pond of prose, sucking the blood of words. 
12. Avoid fancy word. Do not be tempted by a twenty-dollar word when there is a ten-center handy, ready and able. The line between the fancy and the plain, between the atrocious and felicitous, is sometimes alarmingly fine. 
	1. The question of ear is vital. There are times when the ear not only guides us through difficult situation but also saves us from minor or major embarrassments of prose. The ear, for example, must decide when to omit *that* from a sentence, when to retain it. 
13. Unless there is a good reason for its being there, do not inject opinion into a piece of writing. Opinions scatter indiscriminately about leave the mark of egotism on a work. 
14. Do not take shortcuts at the cost of clarity. A good rule is to start your article by writing out names in full, and then, later, when you readers have got their bearings, to shorten them. 
15. The language is perpetually in flux: it is a living stream, shifting, changing, receiving new strength from a thousand tributaries, losing old forms in the backwaters of time. But do not forget that what may seem like pioneering may be merely evasion, or laziness—the disinclination to submit to discipline. 
16. Style takes in its final shape more from attitudes of mind than from principles of composition. No one can write decently who is distrustful of the readers intelligence, or whose attitude is patronising.
