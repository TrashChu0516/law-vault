### Crucial distinctions.
- Be careful about the gateway and the factors, the factors themselves are not the test. Elements vs disjunctive requirements vs factors. 
- Tests (the rule) vs guiding principle (part of the elaboration of the rule) vs underlying justifications (not a direct rule, interpret the situation in the light of this, e.g., policy considerations).
- Implication of a rule vs the rule itself. A is the rule, argue "not A" is implication based on its consequence.

### Structural errors
- Lumping all the facts/rules together. Immediate placement, do not go back and forth.
- Issues - sub-issues - key facts - possibly relevant rules - IRAC hierarchy.
- Errors in rule identification, statement, or explanation.
	- Must have something to apply in the first place instead of application straightaway (building the second floor without the ground floor).
	- That something is usually not the raw general rule, it is often an intermediate product that has a more specific scope. 
	- The goal is for people to follow your reasoning even when they do not agree with your ultimate conclusion. 
	- Conceptual confusion will arise if you lump Elements vs disjunctive requirements vs factors vs relevant considerations in the same group as (a) (b) (c) . 
	- Do not let your readers infer on your logic, show the **threshold analysis** so they know why this applies /not applies.
	- Stating the specific implications without the general statement first.
	- It is ok to paraphrase the case law ratios, but do not misrepresent. The writer should decide whether to include the precedent facts of the ratio (unpack, explain the scope, or not necessary)? It serves the later argument.
		- If clear cut, succinct facts/direct application would do. If controversial (boundary unclear, you need to say more about the facts to explain how the judges drew the line). Complexity of R and complexity of the A usually match each other. 
		- Only delve into the underlying reasons behind the creation of the ratio if you have to. It shifts the narrative from a test to underlying justifications.
		- Where to rationalise the discussion of precedent facts (elucidate the scope of the rule in R vs whether the currents facts are distinct or analogous in A) is not absolute, the goal is to minimise needless repetition. (Always imagine a 3rd party laymen reader without prior research).
	- Use of "see above" is permitted if you believe it is clear for the 3rd party layman reader to follow. If he/she might not find it certain, state the rule again. If there is a different nuance under the same rule, you have to do it again (e.g., to enliven), A's negligence to B might be different from B's negligence to A even though Spandeck governs both. 
	- Confusing one possible way of satisfying a requirement with the requirement itself. ("Sufficient and persistent physical intrusion/encroachment" instead of encroachment by "tree roots" on the land of the another in the tort of continuing nuisance) vs synthesis the rule from the facts (inductive reasoning needed because the precedent does not state the rule clearly).
		-  1st case : State the actual principle then use the precedents facts on which the principle is applied later (when comparing with the facts of the present case).
		- 2nd case: It is necessary to state the facts upfront (rarer as you need multiple facts to draw a credible rule).
	- Negative statement of the rule (exclusionary rule) can't fully substitute the positive rule (stating what doesn't count is not wholly equal to what is required, as the latter is still expected to be mentioned). Unless it is a strategic choice under timed condition that does not require a complete assessment of what is required.  Impairment of your ability to enjoy the use of the land vs I got physically injured but is not directly related. 
- Errors in Application
	- Mismatch between the rule and the application(does not fall under the ambit). Unstated steps of reasoning. Rule will most likely resurface. Have I stated every part of the Rule, did I introduce some other unmentioned rules? 
	- Insufficient consideration of magnitude and whether it meets the threshold. (Do not just compare types and names, delve in). Intuition is the starting point + taking into accounts of precedents (the most severe case that falls below the threshold (the floor)+ the least severe case that passed the threshold (the ceiling) and then compare their facts (and their connections to the underlying general rule) because you can explain and arrive at the range where the actual threshold is sitting in instead of an abstract line of statement).

## Professor's exam-writing advice (distilled from the LRW notes — Wks 3–7)

For use when building answers and when marking drill papers (grill mode).

**Three principles of effective writing (LRW7):**
- **Be clear:** front-load the point; one thought, one sentence, one idea, one paragraph; don't "build up" — use **CREAC** (Conclusion → Rule → Explanation → Application → Conclusion); short sentences; show the links in your reasoning (roadmap along the way, signposting, unpacking implications).
- **Be brief:** omit needless words (Strunk & White).
- **Be memorable:** prefer the active voice; vary verbs judiciously ("supports", "cuts against", "undermines").

**Modifying IRAC for persuasive writing (LRW7):** conclusion up-front; decode the question — **direction words** ("critically assess"), **content words** (the doctrines), **scope/limiting words** ("the extent to which"; "in tension or in harmony"). Steps: scope the question → identify relevant constellations of ideas → identify material → think through interactions → reach a landing (reflected in intro and conclusion).

**Issue-spotting sequence (LRW5):** read the question first; list parties/non-parties; first sweep for similarities; list issues and fit the framework; make reasonable assumptions or run alternative analyses; absence of a fact does not make a point irrelevant — spectrum concepts (degree of closeness etc.) deserve discussion, but genuinely irrelevant material should be omitted; issue-spotting is the most important skill (buried gold) and detail is pegged to the issue's complexity; only go to policy where it is part of the doctrine; write in the frame: **"The issue is whether. In P v D. In this case. As such, the court is likely to find …"**.

**Rule-statement discipline (LRW4):**
- Distinguish: **elements** (all required) vs **disjunctive requirements** (one of) vs **factors** (weighed) vs relevant considerations; **tests** vs guiding principles vs underlying justifications.
- State the rule positively first; negative ("exclusionary") statements cannot wholly substitute.
- Show the **threshold analysis** so the reader knows why a rule applies or not.
- Precedent facts: include/explain them where the boundary is contested; match the complexity of the Rule to the complexity of the Application; state the principle first, then the precedent facts (inductive rules may need facts up-front).
- **Floor/ceiling calibration:** for degree standards, find the most serious case below the line and the least serious case above it, compare facts, and explain where the present facts sit.
- Focus on **disputed** factors, not all factors; state the overall conclusion at the roadmap stage; number paragraphs where allowed.

**Structural errors to avoid:** lumping facts/rules; going back and forth; building application without the intermediate rule; confusing one way of satisfying a requirement with the requirement; mismatch between rule and application; ignoring magnitude/threshold.
